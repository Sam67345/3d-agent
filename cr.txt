SUMMARY
Recent discoveries have revealed remarkable complexity within olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs), including
the existence of two OSN populations based on the expression of Cd36. However, the regulatory mecha-
nisms governing this cellular diversity in the same cell type remain elusive. Here, we show the preferential
expression of 79 olfactory receptors in Cd36+ OSNs and the anterior projection characteristics of Cd36+
OSNs, indicating the non-randomness of Cd36 expression. The integrated analysis of single-cell RNA
sequencing (scRNA-seq) and scATAC-seq reveals that the differences in Cd36+//C0 OSNs occur at the imma-
ture OSN stage, with Mef2a and Hdac9 being important regulators of developmental divergence. We hypoth-
esize that the absence of Hdac9 may affect the activation of Mef2a, leading to the up-regulation of Mef2a
target genes, including teashirt zinc ﬁnger family member 1 ( Tshz1), in the Cd36+ OSN lineage. We validate
that Tshz1 directly promotes Cd36 expression through enhancer bindings. Our study unravels the intricate
regulatory landscape and principles governing cellular diversity in the olfactory system.

The olfactory system plays a critical role in detecting and
discriminating a wide range of odorants. Within the mouse
olfactory epithelium (OE), individual olfactory sensory neurons
(OSNs) selectively express one out of over 1,000 olfactory re-
ceptor (OR) genes,
1,2 resulting in the remarkable ‘‘one-
neuron-one-receptor’’ organization. 3 The speciﬁc OR gene
expression process not only determines odor sensitivity but

glomeruli within the olfactory bulb (OB). 9–12 Recent break-

have unveiled a new dimension of complexity and diversity
within the olfactory system, 4,5,13–18 Cd36, a high-afﬁnity re-
ceptor with binary expression, marked the existence of two
distinct OSN populations in mouse. 4,5,13 As Cd36 binds to
various ligands, including the matrix protein thrombospon-

19 long-chain fatty acids (FAs), 20 oxidized phospholipids, 21
and lipoproteins, 22 it has been thought to play crucial roles in
lipid odor recognition. 23,24 Studies on CD36-deﬁcient mice
have revealed impairments in lipid mixture odor preference
and a drastic reduction in the number of OSNs responding
to oleic acid, a major milk component. 25,26
Despite the preliminary descriptions of transcriptome differ-
ences and the function of the Cd36-expressing OSNs, the regu-
latory mechanisms underlying this distinguished transcriptome
difference remain unclear. Several key questions pertaining to
the binary expression of Cd36 within the OSN populations
remain unrevealed: what determines whether Cd36 is expressed
or not within speciﬁc OSNs? Is there a coordination or interde-
pendence between Cd36 expression and the selection of OR
genes in individual OSNs? How and when does the expression
of Cd36 become divergent and lead to the existence of two
distinct OSN populations? These critical gaps in our understand-
ing of the intricate molecular mechanisms governing cellular di-
versity within the olfactory system call for rigorous investigation.

characterize the transcriptome and epigenome proﬁles of mature
OSNs in the mouse main OE (MOE). By integrating transcriptome

RESULTS
Transcriptome diversity and spatial projection of Cd36+
OSNs
A previous study has demonstrated that the cellular diversity of
the mouse OSN populations can be decomposed into both cell
identity-associated and activity-related gene expression mod-
ules; cellular identity denotes their spatial position in the MOE
and the expression of a lipid receptor.
4 Cd36+O S N se x h i b i ta
signiﬁcantly distinct transcriptome proﬁle compared to Cd36/C0
OSNs.4,5,13 We ﬁrst reproduced and conﬁrmed the transcriptome
proﬁle of the mouse MOE by performing scRNA-seq on 36,693
cells, with or without enrichment for mature olfactory sensory neu-
rons (mOSNs) (STAR Methods; Figures S1A–S1E). Unsupervised
clustering of the mature OSN populations revealed a similar dis-
tance and proportion ofCd36+ OSNs to that reported in the study
by Tsukahara et al.4 (Figures S1Fa n d1A). To increase the statis-
tical power, we integrated the OSNs from these two datasets
(STAR Methods ; Figures S1 G and S1H). Among a total of
48,571 OSNs, 5.5% were Cd36+ OSNs (color in green), while
94.5% were Cd36/C0 OSNs (color in gray) ( Figure 1A).
Next, we performed a comparative analysis of the transcriptome
between Cd36+a n dCd36/C0 OSNs (STAR Methods; Table S1). We
found that the Cd36+ OSN-speciﬁc genes are functionally en-
riched in lipid modiﬁcation, FA transmembrane transport, and
organic acid transport ( Figure 1 B), with representative genes
shown in Figure 1 C. The transcriptome difference observed in
Cd36+ OSNs further supports the previous functional study using
Cd36-deﬁcient mice, which has revealed that Cd36 may facilitate
the detection of lipid odors.
25,26 In addition to these Gene Ontology
(GO) terms, we also identiﬁed an enrichment of Cd36+ OSN up-
regulated genes in the regulation of cytosolic calcium ion concen-
tration ( Figure 1 B). Previous studies have shown that CD36 on
taste bud cells interacts with the FAs to induce intracellular calcium
release from the endoplasmic reticulum.
27 This calcium release
then triggers calcium ﬂux from membrane store-operated calcium
channels, ultimately leading to the release of neurotransmitters.28
It suggests that Cd36, as a lipid-binding receptor, appears to play
important roles in signaling perception and transduction in olfac-
tion, as well as taste. Alongside genes associated with functional
pathways, we identiﬁed two TFs, teashirt zinc ﬁnger family mem-
ber 1 ( Tshz1)a n d Zfp407, which are speciﬁcally expressed in
Cd36+O S N s(Figures 1Ba n dS1I). Conversely, we observed fewer
down-regulated genes in Cd36+ OSNs, showing no functional
enrichment except for deacetylase activity (Figure 1B). One repre-
sentative gene is Hdac9, which encodes histone deacetylase.
In the olfactory system, each OSN expresses a single OR allele
from a large family of over 1,000 genes, enabling the detection and
discrimination of various odorants. We next wonder whether there
exists a coordination or interdependence between Cd36 expres-
sion and the selection of OR genes in individual OSNs. By exam-
ining the OR expression inCd36+a n dCd36/C0OSNs, we evaluated
whether there is a set of ORs speciﬁcally expressed or skewed to-
ward one of the two populations (STAR Methods). Our analysis re-
vealed that out of 945 OR-deﬁned OSN groups containing at least

(Figures S2Aa n d1D; Table S2). For instance, Olfr806 and Olfr553

Cd36+ OSNs, we examined their genomic locations and found a

As complex odors in the environment are recognized by OSNs
and then encoded by the ensemble of olfactory glomeruli, we
next examined the projections of Cd36+ OSNs onto the OB using
the olfactory glomerular map obtained from Wang et al. 13 This
map, comprising 654 glomeruli, was constructed by proﬁling
the spatial positions of 65 glomeruli and training a linear model
to predict the locations of other glomeruli based on transcrip-
tional proﬁles of speciﬁc OSN types. Our analysis revealed that
OSNs with Cd36+ enriched ORs project their axons into the
anterior region of the OB ( Figure 1 G). In the same way, based
on their transcriptome proﬁles, Cd36+ OSNs are also predicted
to project to the anterior regions of the OB ( Figure 1 H). This
observation aligns with the previous immunostaining results,
which showed the absence of CD36
+ glomeruli in the posterior
regions of the OB. 26 Furthermore, our scRNA-seq data revealed
signiﬁcantly higher expression of Plxna1, a well-studied marker
gene for anterior projection, 8,12,29 in Cd36+ OSNs compared to
Cd36/C0 OSNs (Figure 1I). In contrast to the anterior-speciﬁc pro-
jection, the dorsoventral projection reveals no apparent differ-
ence for Cd36+ OSNs (Figures 1H and 1I). The dorsoventral pro-
jection of OSNs is linked to their spatial organization in the OE,
which can be divided into different zones. 30–33 We then investi-
gated how Cd36 expression coordinates with OSN spatial iden-
tity in the MOE. Interestingly, we discovered similar spatial distri-
butions in Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs along the dorsoventral axis,
with a slightly higher ratio of dorsal to ventral in Cd36+ OSNs
compared to Cd36/C0 OSNs (Figures S2D and S2E).
In summary, the speciﬁc molecular characteristics of Cd36+
OSNs reﬂect their function in lipid odor detection. The expres-
sion of Cd36 is independently regulated with the dorsoventral
position of OSNs but is associated with their anterior projection.
Our ﬁndings also indicate that a speciﬁc subset of ORs are co-
expressed with Cd36 in a deterministic and programmed
manner rather than randomly.
Identiﬁcation of TFs associated with chromatin
divergence in Cd36+ OSNs
With a deeper understanding of the molecular characteristics ex-
hibited by Cd36+ OSNs, our next inquiry focused on the mech-
anism underlying this diversity. Single-cell chromatin accessi-
bility proﬁling enables us to investigate the epigenomic
landscape of OSNs at single-cell resolution. We obtained a chro-
matin accessibility proﬁle of 13,158 cells from the mouse OE,
with ﬂuorescence-activated cell sorting enrichment for mature
OSNs ( STAR Methods ; Figures S3 A–S3G). Unsupervised clus-
tering of scATAC-seq data for mature OSNs demonstrated a
comparable divergence between Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs at
the epigenomic level, conﬁrming that transcriptional diversity is
encoded within the chromatin landscape ( Figures 2A, S3H, and
S3I). We explored the differences in accessible regions (or
peaks) between Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs and identiﬁed 311
up-regulated peaks and 181 down-regulated peaks in Cd36+
OSNs (Figure 2 B; Table S3 ). Motif enrichment analysis of these
differentially accessible peaks revealed potential TFs that may
drive the chromatin diversity ( Figures 2 B, S3J, and S3K).
Notably, we identiﬁed that the binding motif of Mef2a, Mef2b ,
Foxd3, Stat2, and Foxj3 were enriched in the up-regulated
peaks, whereas the binding motifs of Pknox2, Pou6f2, Meis2,
Pbx3, and Lhx2
were enriched in the down-regulated peaks of
Cd36+ OSNs ( Figures 2 B, S3J, and S3K). To further describe
which gene in the protein family is the functional gene regulating
the divergence in Cd36+//C0 OSNs, we compared the differential
expression of TFs with the accessibility of their binding motifs.
Intriguingly, most of the TFs that showed motif enrichment in
the differential peaks demonstrated strong divergences in chro-
matin accessibility but displayed barely any differences in
expression levels ( Figures 2C and 2D).
Focusing on the MEF2 family with the most signiﬁcant motif
enrichment, we identiﬁed Mef2a with the highest expression
level compared to Mef2b, Mef2c, and Mef2d (Figures S4 A–
S4D). However, the expression levels of Mef2 genes did not
signiﬁcantly differ between Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs. On the
contrary, noticeable differences in chromatin accessibility were
observed at peaks containing MEF2 binding motifs, as quantiﬁed
by chromVAR Z scores, between Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs
(Figures S4 A–S4D, 2E, and 2F). The TFs that are enriched in
the down-regulation peaks showed the same pattern, such as
Lhx2, the one with consistently high expression levels in both
Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs (Figures 2D, 2G, and 2H). It has been
shown that Lhx2 plays a crucial role in the selection of ORs by
binding at OR enhancers, the ‘‘Greek island,’’ and mediating

Trajectory analysis reveals the temporal expression of
trans-factors during OSN development
The co-expression of Cd36 and a speciﬁc subset of ORs implies
their potential coordinated regulation. To explore this further, we
investigated their coordinated regulation throughout the devel-
opment and maturation of OSNs. In addition, we wonder
whether the TFs that show divergent binding activities in the
mature OSN stage had expression differences at the earlier
stage of development, which may explain the controversy.
To answer these questions and elucidate the timing of the initi-
ation of Cd36+ OSN diversity, we performed trajectory analysis
on cells of the olfactory neuronal lineage ( STAR Methods ;
Figures S5 A and S5B). We identiﬁed a developmental period
prior to OSN maturation, during which OSNs began to express
modest amounts of olfactory marker protein ( Omp) but showed
lower expression of adenylate cyclase 3 ( Adcy3), indicating
that they are a transitional stage of mature OSNs
37 (Figure S5C).
These OSNs were deﬁned as Adcy3/C0 OSNs (Figure S5D). RNA
velocity analysis revealed the developmental trajectory of the ol-
factory neuronal lineage from globose basal cells (GBCs) to
mature OSNs ( Figure 3 A). Furthermore, at the Adcy3/C0 OSN
stage, Cd36+ OSNs were clearly distinguished from Cd36/C0
OSNs (Figures 3A and S5E), and Tshz1 was also signiﬁcantly ex-
pressed in the precursor cells of Cd36+ OSNs at the Adcy3/C0
OSN stage ( Figure 3 B). Adcy3/C0 OSNs with high Tshz1 expres-
sion levels were classiﬁed as precursor cells of Cd36+ OSNs,
while the remaining cells with low or no Tshz1 expression were
assigned as precursor cells of Cd36/C0 OSNs ( STAR Methods ;
Figure S5 F). Consequently, we separated the development tra-
jectories of Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs, respectively ( Figure 3C).
Along the developmental trajectories, we investigated the
expression levels of TFs showing signiﬁcant motif enrichment
in differential accessibility regions between Cd36+ and Cd36/C0
OSNs. Remarkably, we found that Lhx2 is the earliest expressed
regulator that is expressed at the GBC stage and the highest ex-
pressed at the immediate neuronal precursor (INP) stage
(Figures 3 D and 3F). However, there is no signiﬁcant difference
in the expression level of Lhx2 between the two lineages along
the developmental trajectory. Although signiﬁcant differential
accessibility at Mef2a binding sites was observed, Mef2a started
to express at the INP stage and increased along the develop-
ment trajectory, but neither showed any expression difference
between the two lineages ( Figures 3 E and 3F). In contrast to
Lhx2 and Mef2a, the expression levels of Tshz1 increased in
the early developmental stage of INPs and subsequently
declined for most cells in the immature OSN stage, remaining
constantly expressed only in Cd36+ OSN precursors at the
Adcy3/C0 OSN stages, and eventually became speciﬁcally ex-
pressed in Cd36+ OSNs ( Figures 3 B, 3F, and 3G). The expres-
sion divergence of Tshz1 was initiated in the immature OSN
stage, when OR expression was determined.
In combination with the binding activities of these three TFs, we
found that both binding activities and expression levels tend to be
higher in the early developmental stage of OSNs (such as the INP
stage) (Figures 3G and 3H). Gradually reaching the mature OSN
stage, Mef2a and Tshz1 are more active in Cd36+ OSNs, and
Lhx2 is more active in Cd36/C0 OSNs ( Figure 3 H). These results
showed that temporal differential expression oftrans-factors dur-
ing OSN development could not explain their binding activity dif-
ferences, suggesting extraordinary regulatory mechanisms
driving the TF binding divergence in OSN populations.
Key regulators of cell fate determination in Cd36+ OSNs
and Cd36/C0 OSNs
Intriguingly, both Lhx2 and Mef2a exhibited continuous expres-
sion beginning at the immature OSN stage and did not show
obvious expression differences between Cd36+ and Cd36/C0
OSNs ( Figures 3 D–3F). How trans-factors with homogeneous
expression play divergent roles in the OSN populations is of
great interest.

In the transcriptome analysis, we found that Hdac9, which is
enriched in deacetylase activity GO terms, was signiﬁcantly
more highly expressed in Cd36/C0 OSNs ( Figure 1 B). Along the
trajectory analysis, we found that Hdac9 was robustly expressed
in a lineage-speciﬁc manner at the mature stage of Cd36/C0 OSNs
(Figure 4 A). By immunoﬂuorescence analysis, HDAC9 protein
was indeed signiﬁcantly higher in Cd36/C0 OSNs compared with
Cd36+ OSNs ( Figures 4 B and 4C). Furthermore, we found a
Cd36/C0 OSN-speciﬁc peak on the intron region of Hdac9,
located 86,750 bp downstream of the Hdac9 promoter and over-
lapping with the Lhx2 chromatin immunoprecipitation-seq peak
signal ( Figure 4 D). Peak-gene linkage analysis also showed
that this enhancer peak has the strongest linkage with Hdac9
expression (Figure 4D). These results indicate that Lhx2 may pro-
mote the expression of Hdac9 in Cd36/C0 OSNs. To conﬁrm this
regulatory relationship, we analyzed bulk RNA-seq datasets
from Lhx2 knockout mice 35 and revealed a signiﬁcant down-
regulation of Hdac9 expression in mature OSNs in the absence
of Lhx2 (Figure 4E).
Hdac9, as a class II histone deacetylase, is speciﬁcally ex-
pressed in Cd36/C0 OSNs, implying its potential role in epigenet-
ically repressing genes that contribute to Cd36+ OSN diversity.
Besides, previous studies have shown that Hdac9 can interact
with Mef2a and function as a transcriptional corepressor to sup-
press the activity of Mef2a and reduce the expression of its target
genes.38–41 The lineage-speciﬁc expression of Hdac9 might
explain the regulatory and binding diversity of Mef2a. In conjunc-
tion with Hdac9 expression, we found a negative correlation be-
tween the TF activity of Mef2a and Hdac9 expression (Figures 4F
and 4G). In conclusion, our results indicate that the different ex-
pressions of Hdac9 might contribute to the activation difference
of Mef2a in Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs.
Regulatory networks and key regulators in promoting
lineage-speciﬁc expression of Cd36
We further built a gene regulatory network (GRN) for Cd36+
OSNs ( STAR Methods ; Figure 5 A), which identiﬁed Mef2a and
Tshz1 as the key regulators for Cd36+ OSN up-regulated genes.
Interestingly, we found that Tshz1 is also regulated by Mef2a via
enhancer binding (Figure 5A). When we examined the chromatin
accessibility proﬁles around the Tshz1 gene, we found a Cd36+
OSN-speciﬁc enhancer region containing the Mef2a binding
motif and exhibiting the strongest linkage with Tshz1 expression
(Figure 5B).
Tshz1 demonstrated remarkable expression speciﬁcity in
Cd36+ OSNs compared to other trans-regulators. To validate
the speciﬁc expression of Tshz1 in Cd36+ OSNs, we performed
RNAscope in situ hybridization and observed effective co-local-
ization of Tshz1 ﬂuorescence signals (in red) with Cd36 in YFP+
OSNs ( Figure 5 C). As expected, Cd36 was abundantly ex-
pressed on the surface of the OE, consistent with previous ob-
servations
42 through immunoﬂuorescence staining. Approxi-
mately 5% of YFP+ OSNs exhibited Cd36 signals ( Figure 5 C),
conﬁrming the proportion of Cd36+ OSNs determined by
scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq.
The GRN analysis identiﬁed that Tshz1 directly regulated the
marker gene Cd36. In the trajectory analysis, we also observed
that Cd36 expression occurred after the expression of Tshz1
(Figure 3 G). We further identiﬁed the cis-regulatory elements
(CREs) of Cd36, which contain Tshz1 binding motifs, showing
a strong linkage with Cd36 expression ( Figure 5 D). To verify
that Tshz1 binds to CREs and drives Cd36 expression, we con-
ducted a CRE reporter assay in vitro for two cis-elements with
Tshz1 binding sites (e1 and e3) and one without a Tshz1 binding
site (e2 as negative control) ( STAR Methods; Figure 5E).
Brieﬂy, we constructed two types of plasmids. One enabled
the overexpression of Tshz1 with its expression monitored by
GFP, and the other was a reporter plasmid with one of the three
potential Cd36 enhancer sequences inserted upstream of the
minimal promoter of the reporter gene (labeling with mCherry).
Subsequently, we co-transfected the overexpression and re-
porter plasmids into the Neuro2a cell line. After 48 h, the expres-
sion of the reporter gene (mCherry) in different cells was de-
tected by ﬂow cytometry. As expected, when Tshz1 was
overexpressed, the mean ﬂuorescence intensity of mCherry
was signiﬁcantly increased in Tshz1-GFP+/mCherry+ dual-
plasmid co-transfected cells compared to those transfected
with empty plasmid (GFP) and reporter plasmids ( Figures 5 F,
S6A, and S6B). Furthermore, when Tshz1 was overexpressed,
e1 and e3 containing the Tshz1 motif exhibited signiﬁcantly
higher expression levels of mCherry reporter compared to that
of e2 without the Tshz1 motif (Figures 5F and 5G). These ﬁndings
support the notion that Tshz1 is capable of driving Cd36 expres-
sion through its interactions with speciﬁc CREs. We have identi-
ﬁed multiple CREs in OSNs that are responsible for mediating the
regulatory effect of Tshz1 on Cd36 expression. Tshz1 plays a
crucial role in promoting Cd36
expression and may drive the
cellular diversity of OSNs. Collectively, we systematically re-
vealed the regulatory relationship, composited with TFs, cis-ele-
ments, and their corresponding target genes, in the regulation of
cellular diversity of OSNs.

OSNs. Intriguingly, we found that the majority of the TFs revealed
by the motif enrichment analysis were not speciﬁc or highly ex-
pressed in the Cd36+ OSN population. This ﬁnding contrasts

Previous studies showed that cyclic adenosine monophos-
phate (cAMP) acts as a key second messenger in signal trans-
duction pathways, which is essential for axon guidance and
neuronal plasticity in OSNs.
8,12,43,44 It has been shown that
cAMP signaling can modulate the interaction between HDAC
(class II histone deacetylases) and MEF2 TFs, playing a critical
role in regulating the expression of MEF2 target genes. 45–48 In
the transcriptome comparison, we found the expression of Neu-
ropilin1 ( Nrp1) is lower in Cd36+ OSNs, indicating lower basal
cAMP levels in Cd36+ OSNs ( Figure S7F).
8,12,44,49 Furthermore,
we also found that Adcy3, an enzyme for cAMP synthesis, ex-
hibits expression differences between Cd36+ and Cd36/C0
OSNs ( Figure S7 G). These results indicate that the cAMP-level
differences in OSNs may also contribute to the interaction be-
tween Hdac9 and Mef2a, which may further contribute to the
transcriptome and epigenome diversity of Cd36+ and Cd36/C0
OSNs. Furthermore, apart from the cAMP level, calmodulin-
stimulated protein phosphatase and calcium-dependent kinase
have been reported to regulate MEF2A activation.
50–52 Interest-
ingly, we observed three of these genes showing differential
expression between Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSN populations, sug-
gesting other potential regulation of MEF2A at the post-transla-
tional level ( Figure S7 H). The signaling-dependent modiﬁcation
and activation of trans-factors may resolve the limitation on
cellular diversity imposed by the number of encoded TFs,
requiring further investigations. These potential post-transla-
tional modiﬁcations of MEF2 and HDAC9 could be further as-
sayed and compared by protein mass spectrometry when the
two OSN cell populations can be separated. These ﬁndings will
shed light on the mechanisms driving cellular diversity in the
mouse OSNs and provide valuable insights into the complex reg-
ulatory processes underlying complex organ development and
function.
Based on our analysis, we propose that the divergence be-
tween Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs might be initiated prior to the
immature OSN stage, coordinated with the choice of OR genes.
This means that the same progenitor cells can give rise to both
Cd36+ and Cd36/C0 OSNs. As we demonstrated, the two popula-
tions are distinguished by the expression of Tshz1 after the
immature OSN stage. However, Tshz1 is not only expressed in
the Cd36+ OSN lineage but also during the INP stage of the com-
mon progenitor cells of Cd36+ OSNs and Cd36/C0 OSNs. There-
fore, genetic labeling in vivo to monitor Tshz1 expression is not
suitable for determining the early precise lineage-associated
cells of Cd36+ OSNs. However, as the lineage relationship for
OSNs has been well established, our study has pinpointed the
developmental stage when the differences emerge through ex-
isting analysis methods, including RNA velocities and gene
expression speciﬁcity. Although traditional genetic labeling ap-
proaches cannot directly monitor the process, single-cell line-
age-tracing approaches
53 interrogating both cell states and line-
age histories will provide new opportunities to understand the
process of lineage determination during OSN development.

Besides the question of how cellular diversity is regulated,
another intriguing question is how cellular diversity evolved.
We further investigated the expression patterns of Cd36 and
Tshz1 in olfactory mucosa across multiple species, including
mice, rats, dogs, marmosets, and humans. 54 We observed a
strong correlation in the expression levels of Cd36 and Tshz1
across these species. Speciﬁcally, the expression levels are
highest in rodents, while they are lower in canids and primates.
(Figure S7I). These ﬁndings provide independent evidence sup-
porting the role of Tshz1 in regulating the expression of Cd36.
Furthermore, the species-speciﬁc expression patterns of Cd36
and Tshz1 imply that the function of Cd36+ OSNs may be spe-
ciﬁc to rodents, highlighting potential evolutionary differences
in olfactory systems among species. The absence or reduced
expression of Cd36 and Tshz1 in the OE in other species (such
as dogs, marmosets, and humans) suggests that Cd36+ OSNs
may be absent or less abundant compared to mice. The two sce-
narios could not be distinguished with bulk RNA-seq data alone.
Large-scale single-cell proﬁling studies in other species are
necessary to investigate the conservation of the Cd36+ OSN
population, and comparative analysis across multiple species
will provide further insights into the evolution of cellular diversity
and its relationship to species-speciﬁc adaptations.
Limitations of the study
There remain avenues to pursue further experiments to elucidate
the exact regulation mechanisms orchestrating the cellular diver-
gence of OSNs. These crucial investigations will be the focus of
future research, particularly with the availability of mouse models
with conditional loss- or gain-of function manipulations of key
regulators, in combination with lineage-tracing and genomics
approaches that could simultaneously assay of cell fate, cell his-
tory, and TF binding at single-cell resolution.
55,56 Additionally,
the Cd36+ OSN population represents a minority ( /C245%)
compared to Cd36/C0 OSNs, potentially limiting the statistical po-
wer for estimating OR preference. This may result in false nega-
tives, where biased ORs are mistakenly identiﬁed as non-biased
ORs. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate how the non-
random selection of a subset of OR genes is achieved, as well as
to conﬁdently identify this subset of OR genes.

Identiﬁcation of Cd36+ enriched OR genes
To increase statistical power, we used integrated scRNA-seq data containing mature OSNs from this study and Tsukahara et al.
4 to
identify Cd36+ enriched OR genes. We ﬁrst identiﬁed the OR gene expressed in each mature OSN from integrated scRNA-seq data.
Mature OSNs from Tsukahara et al. 4 which were found to contradict the previously published annotations of expressed OR genes
were removed from this analysis. All cells expressing the same OR gene were considered as an OSN subgroup. 945 OSN subgroups
with at least 10 cells in each subgroup were identiﬁed and used in this analysis. For each OSN subgroup, we calculated the number of
cells that were found within Cd36+ OSNs and Cd36- OSNs and performed a binomial test to determine if the expressed OR gene was
biased toward any of the two groups. In the scRNA-seq data analyzed, there were 2573 Cd36+ OSNs and 44829 Cd36- OSNs. If the
expressed OR gene was not biased toward any of the two groups, the proportion of cells in this OSN subgroup that were found within
Cd36+ OSNs and Cd36- OSNs should be the same as the proportion of cells in Cd36+ OSNs and Cd36- OSNs (2573/44829 z 0.057
in our case). We used the Benjamini-Hochberg method to correct multiple testing. OSN subgroups with Benjamini-Hochberg cor-
rected p-value <0.05 are regarded as biased OSN subgroups. Among them, we distinguished those predominantly composed of
Cd36+ OSNs and considered their expressed OR genes as Cd36+ enriched OR genes. In contrast, the expressed OR genes of sub-
groups primarily consisting of Cd36- OSNs were categorized as Cd36+ depleted OR genes. To examine if Cd36+ enriched OR genes
were enriched in a particular chromosome, a binomial test for each chromosome was performed. If Cd36+ enriched OR genes were
not enriched in the chromosome, the proportion of Cd36+ enriched OR genes in this chromosome and all chromosomes should be
the same as the proportion of OR genes in this chromosome and all chromosomes.

scATAC-seq data processing and cell type identiﬁcation
For each scATAC-seq dataset, raw reads were preprocessed using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger ATAC 69 version 2.0.0, including read
ﬁltering, alignment to Mus musculus reference genome mm10 provided by 10X Genomics, peak calling, cell calling, and generating
peak-barcode count matrix. The count data and fragment ﬁles were loaded into R (Version 4.1.3) and further processed using the R
package Siganc.70 For each scATAC-seq data, we calculated the nucleosome signal (the ratio of mononucleosomal to nucleosome-
free fragments), TSS enrichment score, and the percentage of reads in peaks for each cell. High-quality cells with 5000–40000 frag-
ments, TSS enrichment score R 4, nucleosome signal <2, and the percentage of reads in peaks R 40 were retained for downstream
analysis. Next, scATAC-seq datasets across two different time points were integrated using the integration workﬂow ( https://
stuartlab.org/signac/articles/integrate_atac.html). First, the peaks called in each dataset were merged using the reduce() function
of the GenomicRanges 71 package to create a common peak set. Next, the count matrix for each dataset was constructed using
this common peak set. To reduce dimensions, latent semantic indexing (LSI) was performed for each dataset. LSI involves two steps.
The ﬁrst step is to normalize data using the frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) transformation by RunTFIDF() function.
Subsequently, the features present in more than 20 cells were used to run singular value decomposition (SVD) on the TF-IDF matrix.
To integrate the two datasets and remove the batch effect, we identiﬁed integration anchors between the two datasets using recip-
rocal LSI projection by the FindIntegrationAnchors() functions and used these anchors to integrate LSI embeddings across the two
datasets using the IntegrateEmbeddings() function. Finally, LSI components 2 to 30 were used to perform uniform manifold approx-
imation and projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction and clustering at a resolution of 0.8. It yielded 22 clusters. To annotate each
cluster, the activity of each gene was quantiﬁed by assessing the chromatin accessibility associated with each gene and these gene
activity scores were normalized. Besides, the pre-processed integrated scRNA-seq dataset was used for cross-modality integration
and label transfer, which also helped to interpret the identity of each cluster. By examining both gene activity scores of previously
mentioned marker genes and predicted labels, we ﬁrst annotated cells that do not belong to the olfactory neuronal lineage: susten-
tacular cells, ensheathing glia, bowman’s gland, periglomerular cells, brush cells, B cells, erythrocytes, basophils, neutrophils, and
monocytes. Due to the incapability to distinguish HBCs and GBCs based on the above preliminary clustering, cell clusters that pu-
tatively corresponded to cells in the olfactory neuronal lineage (HBCs, GBCs, INPs, Immature OSNs, and mature OSNs) were ex-
tracted for further subclustering analysis. Peaks calling was performed using data from cells in the olfactory neuronal lineage using
the CallPeak() function. These peaks were used to generate a count matrix for cells in the olfactory neuronal lineage of each dataset.

Differentially accessible peaks between Cd36+ OSNs and Cd36- OSNs were identiﬁed using a logistic regression model with the
total number of counts in each cell included as a latent variable by the FindMarkers() function. Only those with absolute fold change
>0 and Bonferroni adjusted p-value <0.05 were considered as differentially accessible peaks. Hypergeometric tests were used to test
for enrichment of each DNA motif in the set of differentially accessible peaks compared to a background set of peaks with matched
GC content by the FindMotifs() function. To further identify differentially active motifs between cell types, motif activities for each cell
were measured by running ChromVAR. After integrating with mature OSNs scRNA-seq data, peak-to-gene linkage analysis was per-
formed by computing the correlation between gene expression and accessibility at peaks within 500 kb of the transcription start si-

To construct the cell type-speciﬁc gene regulatory network (GRN), we ﬁrst selected candidate transcription factors (TFs) based on
the results of motif enrichment analysis for differentially accessible peaks, differential ChromVAR analysis and TF expression levels.
Speciﬁcally, candidate key TFs were chosen based on the following criteria: their motifs exhibited signiﬁcant enrichment within cell-
type-speciﬁcally up-regulated peaks (p-value <0.05 and fold enrichment R1.5), their ChromVAR activities were signiﬁcantly up-regu-
lated (adjusted p-value <0.05 and absolute differences R0.4), and they displayed positive gene expression in the given cell type.
Next, we searched the presence of the candidate TF motifs within the cell-type-speciﬁcally up-regulated peaks. Subsequently,
we identiﬁed potential target genes of these peaks by peak-to-gene linkage analysis. Only positive region–target gene links are
kept. Each GRN is visualized by Cytoscape (version 3.9.1).

Bulk RNA-seq data analysis for wild-type and Lhx2 KO mOSNs
We downloaded raw bulk RNA-seq data of wild-type and Lhx2 KO mOSNs from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession
number GSE93570 (2017). 35 For each bulk RNA-seq dataset, fastp 75 version 0.21.0 was used to remove adapters and low-quality
reads. Trimmed reads were aligned to Mus musculus reference genome mm10 using STAR version 2.5.2. 76 Uniquely aligning reads
with alignment quality above 30 were retained for downstream analysis. Differential gene expression analysis between wild-type and
Lhx2 KO mOSNs was performed in R with the DESeq2 package. 77
