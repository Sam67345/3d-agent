Foundation models (FMs) are reshaping computational biology by learning transferable representations from 
single-cell and spatial omics data, yet their usage in actual routine bioinformatics has not lived to the 
prediction. A simple reason is the wide gap between tool development and clientele. Users’ guides (or 
consumer reports) may bridge the gap. Here, we present a user-centric guide to seven FMs—scGPT, 
scFoundation, CellPLM, Geneformer, SCimilarity, Nicheformer, and SToFM. The primary evaluation 
focuses on three practically supported tasks: batch correction, cell-type annotation, and cellular 
differentiation reconstruction, the latter of which was explored in both zero-shot and fine-tuned settings. 
Two additional tasks, GRN inference and gene perturbation prediction, are included to inform readers of the 
current capability boundaries in the field. For all tasks, we followed the protocols described in each model’s 
original publication and official repository. In addition to downstream performance, we systematically assess 
the documentation and tutorials, installation difficulty, stability, memory usage, and GPU compatibility. 
Current FMs perform well on relatively static tasks, but remain limited in recovering dynamic lineage 
relationships and gene regulatory networks. Their practical use is further constrained by fragile software 
environments and inefficient resource management. We also show that LLM-assisted agent-like workflows 
can reduce barriers to practical use by automating code writing, revision, and dependency analysis. While 
knowledge-informed FMs, coupled with robust agentic tools, are expected to make FMs more powerful, 
users’ guides may make them genuinely usable in biological research. 
In the life sciences, AI foundation models (FMs) have the promise (not fully realized yet) of marking a 
paradigm shift. Originally developed for language and vision tasks, Transformer-based models are now 
enabling new ways to interpret biological data, capturing transferable representations that generalize across 
datasets, species, and modalities. In the single-cell and spatial transcriptomics fields, FMs are redefining 
how researchers explore cellular heterogeneity, tissue organization, and regulatory interactions by learning 
universal molecular embeddings from large-scale data1-7.  
However, the practical usability of these models in everyday bioinformatics workflows remains 
underexplored. Existing benchmark studies have largely focused on algorithmic performance, while 
neglecting critical real-world factors—installation difficulty, documentation completeness, GPU 
compatibility, and error handling8,9. In reality, even experienced users often struggle to set up environments, 
revised codes for new datasets, or adapt pretrained models to novel tasks. Meanwhile, the increasing 
accessibility of large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT10 and Codex11 has opened new possibilities 
for AI-assisted workflow support. In routine FM-related operations, these tools may help improve efficiency,

To address this gap, we propose a user-centric evaluation framework to assess the practical performance, 
usability, and adaptability of single-cell and spatial FMs. By combining empirical benchmarking with 
real-user studies and LLM-assisted adaptation, this work seeks to bridge the gap between algorithmic 
innovation and its tangible impact in bioinformatics practice. Our goal is to advance the next phase of 
AI-driven biology—where models are not only accurate but also usable, reproducible, and accessible to the 
broader life-science community. 
Study design and evaluation protocol. We recruited six participants from computer science, mathematics, 
and biology across undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral levels to evaluate seven representative FMs: five 
for single-cell omics data (scGPT1, scFoundation2, Geneformer3, CellPLM4, and SCimilarity5) and two for 
spatial omics data (Nicheformer6 and SToFM7) (Fig.1a). These models were chosen because they are either 
recently published or widely regarded as strong performers in existing benchmarks8,9. Details on each 
model’s accessibility and published date are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Participants performed 
five tasks—batch-effect correction, cell-type annotation, differentiation characterization, GRN inference, 
and gene perturbation prediction—on three GPU platforms (RTX 4090, L40, and RTX PRO 6000). They 
documented each step in detail, from environment setup to downstream analysis, including runtime, GPU 
memory usage, and task outcomes. Importantly, all participants were allowed to use LLMs (e.g., Codex11, 
ChatGPT10, and Gemini12) to assist with debugging, code writing, and code revision, thereby simulating 
real-world research practice. The LLM-assisted workflow was included solely to facilitate the practical use 
of FMs and to reflect realistic usage scenarios, rather than as a formal component of the modeling 
framework.  
Software usability and developer maintenance. High-quality tutorials, reproducible environment setup, 
and timely responses from developers are essential for the practical application of FMs. All models were 
evaluated using the same metrics and procedures, following the recommended settings in the original 
publications. In addition, participants were free to evaluate the models in any order, thereby reducing 
potential order effects. To improve transparency, we also visualized the raw records for each participant. 
According to user’s evaluation, SCimilarity and CellPLM provided the smoothest installation experience, 
featuring detailed tutorials and self-contained dependencies (Fig.1b, c). In contrast, models such as scGPT, 
scFoundation, Geneformer, Nicheformer, and SToFM presented numerous challenges, including missing 
setup scripts and strict version dependencies. Moreover, scGPT failed to compile on the latest NVIDIA 
architectures due to conflicts between PyTorch and torchtext libraries. Although scGPT provided a web 
interface, its functionality remained limited for complex analyses. In terms of maintenance—evaluated 
based on the responsiveness of issue handling on GitHub and Hugging Face—Geneformer stood out for its 
strong developer support, with issues being actively triaged and addressed on a near-daily basis. In contrast, 
the other models generally showed lower levels of maintainer engagement and slower issue resolution. 
Overall, these findings underscore the need for standardized deployment pipelines and sustained 
maintenance to improve reproducibility and compatibility. 
Batch effect correction. Batch effect correction is essential in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics 
because technical differences across platforms, chemistries, runs, or slides can mimic biology and inflate 
false positives, including in perturbation inference. Intuitively, it “recalibrates” datasets to a common 
baseline—like correcting for different lighting—so comparable cell states or tissue regions align, while true 
biological signals (cell identities, trajectories, and spatial structure) are preserved. 
This task evaluated how effectively each FM mitigates batch effects on single-cell dataset Immune Cell 
Atlas and spatial omics dataset DLPFC (Supplementary Table 2). Using pretrained weights, we conducted 
zero-shot inference and ranked models by ASW-batch scores. [Note - Zero-shot refers to a model's ability to

SCimilarity and Nicheformer exhibited superior batch-correction performance, with SCimilarity providing 
the best tutorials and scripts for obtaining cell embeddings (Fig.1b, h). In contrast, scFoundation, 
Nicheformer, and SToFM required extensive manual preprocessing (e.g., gene order), and Geneformer also 
contained hard-coded parameters (n_count) that further reduced portability. These results are largely 
consistent with prior benchmark8: SCimilarity remains competitive, whereas CellPLM tends to rank lower 
on immune atlas datasets. By contrast, scGPT performs worse in our evaluation than previously reported. 
This discrepancy likely stems from differences in evaluation protocols: we used a zero-shot learning setting, 
whereas the prior study assessed scGPT under fine-tuning8. 
To address these challenges, we explored LLM-assisted code refactoring as one practical strategy, using 
tools such as Codex to automatically analyze, generate, and revise code, and help resolve execution errors 
(Supplementary Note 1), thereby providing practical guidance for users. This substantially improved 
reproducibility and reduced debugging time. Overall, although FMs perform well technically, LLM-based 
assistance can greatly enhance usability, automation, and accessibility. 
Cell type annotation. Cell type annotation is a foundational step in the analysis of single-cell and spatial 
transcriptomics data, as it assigns each cell (or spot) an interpretable biological identity based on its gene 
expression profile. Without reliable annotation, downstream analyses—such as differential expression, and 
cell–cell communication—can be easily misinterpreted or driven by technical noise rather than true biology. 
Following each FM’s tutorial, we added a classification head and fine-tuned the model on the HumanPBMC 
single-cell RNA-seq dataset and the DLPFC spatial transcriptomics dataset (Supplementary Table 2), with 
performance evaluated by accuracy and recall. SCimilarity performs better in our evaluation than in prior 
benchmarks8, likely because we applied full fine-tuning, whereas earlier studies evaluated it in a zero-shot 
setting. By contrast, scGPT ranks lower in our benchmark. This drop appears to be driven by compatibility 
issues with modern high-performance GPUs, which prevented full fine-tuning and forced us to adopt a 
partial fine-tuning strategy (unfreezing only a subset of layers). While CellPLM provided the most 
user-friendly scripts for obtaining results (Fig.1c, i). Nicheformer, however, lacked support for classification, 
as its fine-tuning scripts were designed for regression tasks. Despite these differences, all single-cell FMs 
performed well after fine-tuning, consistently maintaining high accuracy (mostly above 0.9), and the 
difference between models were not significant.  
Participants employed LLMs to customize fine-tuning scripts, and we found that targeted prompts (for 
example, asking Codex to compare the training logic of scFoundation and Nicheformer) were substantially 
more effective than generic instructions. However, the usefulness of LLMs depended strongly on prompt 
specificity, and key decisions regarding model logic and architecture still required human judgment 
(Supplementary Note 2 provides practical guidance). We also observed marked differences in memory 
efficiency across models: scGPT required more than 40 GB memory for full fine-tuning, whereas 
scFoundation used more than 96 GB under its default settings; notably, this could be reduced to 
approximately 12 GB by adding a GPU cache-clearing step. Finally, most FMs lacked built-in visualization 
functions, requiring users to generate plots manually, which further underscores the need for more 
interactive and interpretable tool design.  
Cellular differentiation tree reconstruction. Cellular differentiation tree reconstruction builds an 
interpretable “roadmap” of how cells transition from early to mature states in single-cell and spatial 
transcriptomics data. Because many tissues—especially tumors—contain continuous, transitional states that 
are not well captured by discrete labels, this analysis orders cells along trajectories and branching points to 
reveal state changes and lineage progression across conditions and spatial contexts.

We further explored whether FMs can capture cell lineage relationships using simulated continuous 
single-cell and spatial omics data generated by scMultiSim 13 (Fig. 2a, b). The embeddings from FMs serve 
as cell representations that capture the structure of the data distribution. In this space, proximity between 
embeddings reflects similarity along a continuous state manifold, offering a proxy for lineage relationships. 
Under zero-shot learning, all models produced highly mixed embeddings with little separation between cell 
stages. In contrast, fine-tuning with class labels markedly improved both clustering and lineage inference. 
Among the fine-tuned models, scFoundation performed best, SCimilarity ranked second, and CellPLM 
ranked third (Fig. 2c, d), while most other FMs still failed to recover the underlying differentiation 
relationships. Thus, although many FMs are effective for static classification, they generally struggle to 
characterize evolutionary continuity, especially in the zero-shot setting, highlighting a key challenge for the 
field. 
Gene perturbation prediction. Such evaluations will require detailed analyses currently in progress. The 
perturbation task predicts post-treatment transcriptional profiles. Only scGPT and scFoundation provided 
runnable scripts for fine-tuning; for the remaining models, we were unable to establish usable fine-tuning 
pipelines for this task. We therefore followed the official procedures described in the scGPT and 
scFoundation GitHub repositories, respectively, and evaluated performance using both mean squared error 
(MSE) and Pearson correlation. The evaluation results show that scFoundation converges faster and achieves 
higher predictive accuracy than scGPT. Its standardized shell scripts and clear documentation allowed for 
the reproduction of experiments by specifying data paths, drastically reducing setup time. In contrast, 
scGPT’s missing dependency package (e.g., gears) impeded installation. These results emphasize the value 
of end-to-end executable pipelines and detailed documentation in reducing barriers to model deployment. 
Gene regulatory network inference. Gene regulatory network inference goes beyond marker genes to 
identify the regulators that drive cell states in single-cell and spatial transcriptomics data. By modeling 
regulatory relationships between transcription factors and target genes, it explains why cell types or tissue 
regions activate distinct programs and highlights candidate master regulators linked to disease or treatment 
response. We next tested whether FMs could recover a predefined GRN using simulated data generated by 
scMultiSim13 (Fig. 2a, b). We treated the gene embeddings produced by FMs as representations learned from 
data, and assumed that proximity in the embedding space captures functional or statistical associations rather 
than direct regulatory or causal relationships 1,2. Although such proximity does not by itself imply regulation, 
it may still provide useful signal for distinguishing true transcription factor and target pairs from unrelated 
gene pairs. At the same time, directly computing similarity from embeddings is a simplified strategy and 
may not fully exploit the information encoded in these vectors 2. Therefore, to enable fair comparisons 
across models, we adopted a unified GRN inference framework in which a discriminator was trained on 
gene embeddings to predict edge probabilities. Performance was then evaluated by AUROC using the 
ground-truth GRN of the simulated data. Only scGPT, scFoundation, and Geneformer provided usable 
workflows. scGPT’s gene embeddings primarily reflected broad biological priors that were largely 
independent for the input data, whereas scFoundation and Geneformer produced embeddings that were 
strongly dependent on the specific input data. Although scGPT achieved the highest reconstruction metrics, 
AUROC values hovered around 0.5—essentially random. Fine-tuning improved metrics by ~20–30% 
(Fig.1j), underscoring the importance of supervised learning, but FM-based GRN inference remains 
unreliable and of limited practical use. This highlights the gap between data-driven embedding similarity 
and true mechanistic understanding.  
Conclusion - Transformer-based FMs are redefining bioinformatics by learning general-purpose molecular 
representations. Through a consumer-oriented evaluation, we demonstrate that while current FMs can 
capture meaningful biological structure, their usability and adaptability remain major bottlenecks. Fragile 
software environments, incomplete documentation, and inefficient resource management frequently

undermine the reproducibility of these models in practice. LLM-assisted code writing and modification 
offers a promising solution: from an agentic perspective, LLMs such as Codex can act as lightweight 
orchestrators—automating environment setup, script adaptation, and error handling—to make FMs more 
accessible to non-expert users. We acknowledge the importance of standardized workflows and 
configuration records for improving reproducibility and practical usability (Supplementary Note 3); these 
resources will be further consolidated in our future work and released more comprehensively in a structured 
repository. 
Beyond usability, our results highlight a conceptual limitation: most current FMs are optimized for static 
prediction but not for relational or dynamic biology 14. They can classify and integrate cells effectively, yet 
fail to reconstruct gene regulatory or lineage relationships, reflecting a shallow understanding of causality 
and temporal dependencies. A substantial body of work suggests that current AI models still do not achieve 
sufficiently accurate prediction of GRNs or perturbation responses for reliable practical use 8,9,15,16. At 
present, no model can be confidently recommended for real-world applications in these tasks. We will 
further investigate the underlying reasons in a follow-up study. As an illustrative example, when using 
scFoundation for the cell-type annotation task, two participants required 12 and 18 rounds of effective 
interaction, respectively, to get the pipeline running with ChatGPT and Gemini. Although these counts do 
not constitute a formal benchmark, as they are influenced by prompt quality and prior experience with 
LLMs, they nevertheless suggest that model performance may differ in practice. 
Moreover, we provide model recommendations tailored to three user groups by considering usability, setup 
difficulty, and the diversity of downstream tasks. Specifically, (i) clinicians or experimental biologists with 
limited computational expertise may benefit most from scGPT, whose web-based interface avoids direct 
coding and typically requires only the upload of input data to generate results; (ii) molecular biologists with 
moderate scripting experience may begin with relatively easy-to-configure FMs, such as SCimilarity and 
CellPLM, to become familiar with the overall workflow and key analysis steps; and (iii) computational 
biologists or bioinformaticians may further explore more extensible models, including Geneformer, scGPT, 
and scFoundation (Figs.1, 2). Their stronger developer maintenance can facilitate version updates, 
dependency compatibility fixes, and community support, thereby reducing the long-term costs of 
deployment and reproducibility. With the continued evolution and wider adoption of agent technologies, 
these practical challenges are likely to be alleviated further. 
To advance the field, we advocate for knowledge-informed FMs that explicitly integrate biological 
priors—such as pathways, GRNs, lineage constraints and spatial organization—into their architectures and 
training objectives 17,18. Coupled with agentic LLM tools capable of autonomous code adaptation, data 
validation, and visualization, such models could evolve from isolated algorithms into intelligent research 
companions. By bridging algorithmic innovation with practical usability, this study lays the foundation for a 
more accessible, interpretable, and human-centered era of AI-driven biology.
